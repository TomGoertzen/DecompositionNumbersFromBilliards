Billiards visualisations

Interactive visualisations for the Billiards conjecture and its use in computing three-row decomposition numbers.

Introduction

A long-standing open problem in modular representation theory is to understand the decomposition numbers of symmetric groups. For a partition \(\lambda\) of \(n\), the Specht module \(S^\lambda\) is irreducible in characteristic zero. The decomposition number \(d_{\lambda,\mu}=[S^\lambda:D^\mu]\) is the multiplicity of the simple module \(D^\mu\) as a composition factor in the reduction of \(S^\lambda\) modulo \(p\), where \(\mu\) is a \(p\)-regular partition of \(n\). Results of Donkin [D] and Erdmann [E, §1.4] identify these numbers with Weyl-filtration multiplicities in tilting modules:

\[ d_{\lambda,\mu}=[T_{GL_r}(\mu):\Delta_{GL_r}(\lambda)], \]

provided both partitions have at most \(r\) parts. We focus on partitions with at most three rows, corresponding to polynomial representations of \(GL_3\). On restriction to \(SL_3\), a partition \(\lambda=(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)\) determines the dominant weight

\[ (\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\omega_1+(\lambda_2-\lambda_3)\omega_2. \]

Riche and Williamson [RW15] relate tilting characters to the antispherical \(p\)-Kazhdan--Lusztig polynomial \({}^p{n}_{y,x}\). In regular blocks, we have

\[ [T(w\cdot_p\lambda_0):\Delta(y\cdot_p\lambda_0)]={}^p n_{y,w}(1), \]

where \(\lambda_0\) is a regular weight in the fundamental \(p\)-alcove. Below, we explain how to deduce many decomposition numbers from this equation. Motivated by extensive computations, Lusztig and Williamson [LW] proposed the Billiards conjecture, a discrete dynamical algorithm governed by local rules that predicts certain wall \(p\)-canonical basis elements in type \(A_2\). After further computations of \(p\)-canonical basis elements, Jensen [J] subsequently corrected one of the local rules (merging rule). Computer programs for \(p\)-canonical basis elements and the underlying theory that led to this revised conjecture can be found in [ASLoc] and [GJW23]. We have used these programs to test selected computations on this page. Below we illustrate the corrected conjecture and the range of tilting characters and decomposition numbers accessible using its predictions. The content of the page is largely based on the Honours thesis of Maxwell Liu [Liu] and the references therein.

Range of the Billiards input

Let \(W\) and \(W_f\) be the affine and finite Weyl group of \(SL_3\) respectively, \({}^f W\) be minimal-coset representatives for \(W_f \backslash W\), and \({}^p \underline{N}_x\) and \(N_x\) be \(p\)-canonical and standard basis elements of the antispherical module associated with \(SL_3\), where \(x \in {}^f W\). We can represent \[{}^p \underline{N}_x = \sum_{y \in {}^f W} {}^p n_{y, x} N_y\] denoting \({}^p n_{y, x} \) as antispherical \(p\)-Kazhdan-Lusztig polynomials.

Now, let \(T(\lambda), \Delta(\lambda)\) be the tilting and Weyl characters associated with a dominant weight \(\lambda\) of \(SL_3\) respectively. If \(\lambda_0\) is a regular weight in the fundamental \(p\)-alcove and \(w, y \in {}^f W\), Riche-Williamson [RW15] states \[[T(w \cdot_p \lambda_0): \Delta(y \cdot_p \lambda_0)] = {}^{p}n_{y, w}(1).\] In other words, we can find the decomposition numbers of Weyl modules in tilting modules in terms of the corresponding coefficients of the \(p\)-canonical basis. Moreover, as Weyl characters can be fully calculated for \(SL_3\), this allows us to use \(p\)-canonical basis elements to calculate tilting characters within corresponding alcoves.

The billiards conjecture [LW] calculates conjectural \(p\)-canonical basis elements of the anti-spherical module associated with \(SL_3\) for certain alcoves on the wall. Precisely, it computes \({}^p \underline{N}_{x_i}\) for \(0 \leq i < 2p(p+1)\), where \(x_i\) corresponds to the \((i+1)\)th alcove on the wall of the dominant weight lattice. It thus also provides tilting characters on the wall of the dominant weight lattice in a certain range. Our cached ASLoc checks agree for \(p=3\), \(i=0,\ldots,23\); \(p=5\), \(i=0,\ldots,59\); and \(p=7\), \(i=0,\ldots,30\).

The visualisation below displays all weights that we can find conjectural tilting characters for using the wall data provided by the billiards conjecture. A point \((a,b)\) in the triangular lattice represents the dominant \(SL_3\) weight \(a\omega_1+b\omega_2\). Green points use terminal Billiards wall data inside the expected range \(i < 2p(p+1)\); orange points use wall data after that cutoff and thus cannot solely be calculated using the billiards conjecture.

How this picture is computed

To recursively compute tilting characters, we use the Donkin tensor product theorem to construct tilting modules of higher weights. In order to construct all tilting modules, we need to know all tilting characters in the shifted box \((p-1)\rho+X_{<p}\) and on the walls of the dominant lattice. As the billiards conjecture only calculates conjectural wall \(p\)-canonical basis elements in a given range, the tilting characters we can recursively compute are thus limited.

For a fixed \(p\), the above visualisation classifies dominant \(SL_3\) weights \((a,b)\). The purple square is the shifted box \((p-1)\rho+X_{<p}\), namely \(p-1\leq a,b\leq 2p-2\). The dark blue points are elementary bottom-alcove, first-adjacent alcove, and Steinberg cases. If \(a,b\geq p-1\), we apply the Donkin reduction \[ (a,b)=(p-1+r_1,p-1+r_2)+p(a',b') \] and recursively classify \((a',b')\).

This recursion terminates in a shifted-box, elementary, or terminal wall-strip case. Translation reduces the remaining wall-strip cases to wall input; every required index must satisfy \(i<2p(p+1)\). The colours describe this mathematical reduction range.

Calculating three-row decomposition numbers

Once the relevant tilting character is known, the decomposition number is just the coefficient of \(\Delta(\lambda)\) in \(T(\mu)\). Thus they can be obtained directly after the tilting character is computed, as: \[ [T(\mu):\Delta(\lambda)]=[S^\lambda:D^\mu]. \]

The sliders \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) display three-row partitions and \(p\)-regular three-row partitions of \(n\) respectively, both in dominance order. The \(\lambda\)-slider changes the Specht module \(S^\lambda\) while keeping \(\mu\) fixed; the \(\mu\)-slider changes the simple module \(D^\mu\) while keeping \(\lambda\) fixed. In both cases we only include labels allowed by dominance order/unitriangularity.

Billiards wall dynamics

The formula for conjectural \(p\)-canonical basis elements \({}^p \zeta_i\) in terms of the canonical basis \(\underline{N}_x\) is as follows: \[{}^p \zeta_i := \underline{n}_{x_i} + \sum_{(\mu, n(v^k)) \in \tilde{Z}, n \in \{i, i-1, i-2\}} \varphi(v^k) \underline{n}_{x^s_\mu}.\]

Here, \(\tilde{Z}\) consists of tuples \((\mu, n(v^k))\) where \(\mu\) are weights of \(SL_3\). We can represent each \(n(v^k)\) as "labels" of a weight \(\mu\) on the weight lattice of \(SL_3\). The formula above essentially encodes each label as a "triple" of canonical basis elements, such that each label \(n(v^k)\) corresponds to a canonical basis element in \({}^p \zeta_i\) for \(i = n, n+1, n+2\).

The billiards conjecture calculates this \(\tilde{Z}\) through a \(3\)-step algorithm on the dominant weight lattice of \(SL_3\). The name of the billiards conjecture comes from how the 3rd step of this dynamical algorithm resembles the bouncing of billiard balls in a triangular grid. We use Jensen's corrected merging rule [J].

The following visualisation shows how this dynamical algorithm determines conjectural \(p\)-canonical basis elements on the wall. At time \(i\), the bright points are exactly the labels \(i,i-1,i-2\) that contribute to \({}^p \zeta_i\). When animated, you can see the "billiards"-like behavior of the algorithm.
